It was a wet Tuesday in November, about four months in. I was on a doorstep off Bishopthorpe Road, in front of a terraced house, and a woman in her seventies had been locked out since morning. She stood there in the drizzle with a carrier bag of shopping, holding her cardigan shut. I knelt down and fiddled with the lock for ten minutes or so, then felt the last pin go. The door opened and she let out a little breath, like she'd been holding it all day. She pushed a flask of tea into my hands and said sorry for the trouble.

Standing there, I realised I hadn't thought about the screens, the P&L, or the 7 a.m. dread I'd lugged around London for years. Nobody in the City ever thanked me like that. The money I made there never felt as honestly earned as that tea. I drove home with cold knees and a damp collar, and for the first time in ages I didn't feel like I was waiting for my life to start.
