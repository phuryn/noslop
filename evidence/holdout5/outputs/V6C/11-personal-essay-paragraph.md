It was a wet Tuesday in November, about four months in, and I was on the doorstep of a terraced house off Bishopthorpe Road. A woman in her seventies had been locked out since morning. She'd been standing in the drizzle with a carrier bag of shopping, her cardigan pulled tight around her. I knelt at the door and worked the lock for ten minutes or so, until I felt the last pin set. When it turned, she let out a small breath, like she'd been holding it all day. She pushed a flask of tea into my hands and apologised for the trouble. And I realised I hadn't thought once about the screens, the P&L, or the 7 a.m. dread I'd carried around London for years. Nobody in the City had ever thanked me like that, and none of the money I'd made there had felt as plainly earned as that tea. I drove home with cold knees and a damp collar, and for the first time in a long while, I didn't feel like I was waiting for my life to start.
