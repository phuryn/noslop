On a wet Tuesday in November, about four months in, I was on the doorstep of a terraced house off Bishopthorpe Road. A woman in her seventies had been locked out since the morning. She stood in the drizzle with a carrier bag of shopping and her cardigan pulled tight. I knelt at the door and worked the lock for maybe ten minutes until the last pin set. When it turned, she let out a small breath, as if she'd been holding it all day. She pressed a flask of tea into my hands and apologised for the trouble. Standing there, I realised I hadn't thought about the screens and the P&L, or the 7 a.m. dread I'd carried around London for years. Nobody in the City had ever thanked me like that, and the money I'd made there never felt as plainly earned as that tea. I drove home with cold knees and a damp collar, no longer waiting for my life to start.
