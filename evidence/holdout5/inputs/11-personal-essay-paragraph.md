It happened on a wet Tuesday in November, about four months in, on the doorstep of a terraced house off Bishopthorpe Road. A woman in her seventies had been locked out since the morning, and she'd been standing in the drizzle with a carrier bag of shopping and her cardigan pulled tight. I knelt at the door, worked the lock for maybe ten minutes, and felt the last pin set. When it turned, she let out a small breath, as if she'd been holding it all day. She pressed a flask of tea into my hands and apologised for the trouble. I realised I hadn't thought about the screens, the P&L, or the 7 a.m. dread I'd carried around London for years. Nobody had ever thanked me like that in the City, and the money I'd made there had never felt as plainly earned as that tea. I drove home with cold knees and a damp collar, and for the first time in a long while I didn't feel like I was waiting for my life to start.
