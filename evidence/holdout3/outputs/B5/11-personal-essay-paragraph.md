I was fifty-two, standing in the shallow end of Ladywell Arena at seven on a Tuesday morning with my arms folded across my chest, as if that would make me harder to see. Joan, my instructor, had taught hundreds of people to swim. She treated my fear as a practical problem, not a flaw in my character, and that was somehow the kindest thing she could have done. She never told me to relax. She told me to breathe out under the water, let my feet leave the floor, and trust that the pool would do part of the work.

For weeks I was bad at it in a way I hadn't been bad at anything in years. I was clumsy, I swallowed water, and eight-year-olds glided past me in the next lane. By the end, though, I was surprised by how little I minded. Being a beginner again was humbling, but it was also oddly restful: one morning a week when nobody expected me to know how already. The first time I crossed the width of the pool without touching the bottom, I felt quietly astonished, as if I'd found a room in my own house that had always been there.
