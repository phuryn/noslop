I've been translating Spanish-English legal documents for twelve years, and every few months an agency offers me €0.04 a word to "post-edit" a machine-translated contract, as if that were a discount on the real work rather than a different and worse job. It isn't cheaper. It's the same liability with less of the thinking. A contract is a document where one misplaced modal verb, one false friend like *disposición* rendered as "disposition" instead of "provision," or one quietly dropped negation can shift an obligation from one party to the other. Machine output is fluent enough to hide those errors, which is exactly what makes it dangerous: I can't skim a draft that reads smoothly, I have to verify every clause against the source anyway, and I also have to untangle the machine's confident wrong guesses about terminology, jurisdiction, and legal concepts that have no clean equivalent between the two systems. That means reading everything twice, once to understand what the source says and once to catch where the machine betrayed it, and then still rewriting whatever I can't trust. By the time I've done that, I haven't saved any effort; I've just been paid a third of my rate to carry the same professional responsibility. The client sees a saving on the invoice and imagines the risk has shrunk along with it, but it hasn't. It has simply moved to a cheaper, more rushed, less visible place, and in a contract dispute that's usually where the bill finally shows up.
